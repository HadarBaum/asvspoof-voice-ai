# 10-minute presentation script

Spoken script for the 18-slide deck in `docs/slides/slides.pptx`. Timings are
cumulative and assume ~145 words/minute. Bracketed lines are stage directions,
not spoken. Every number here comes from `docs/training_metrics.json`,
`docs/model_comparison.json`, and the 2,000-utterance streaming run in
`docs/RESULTS.md` — re-check them if you re-run the pipeline before presenting.

**Total: 10:00.** The demo is the only section with slack in it — if you are
running late when you reach it, cut the similar-clips playback, not the
threshold story.

---

## 0:00 — 0:45 · Problem & dataset

> Can you tell whether a voice you just heard was a real person or generated by
> an AI? That's the question this project answers, end to end, on real data.
>
> We use ASVspoof2019 — an anti-spoofing benchmark for speaker verification. It
> ships two partitions, and picking between them was our first real decision.
> LA, Logical Access, is genuine human speech versus speech synthesized by
> nineteen different text-to-speech and voice-conversion systems. PA, Physical
> Access, is a human recording replayed through a loudspeaker.
>
> We use **only LA**, deliberately. "Human or AI" only means something against
> synthetic audio. PA's attacks are a human voice played back — labelling that
> "AI" would have been a false claim, so we left it out rather than inflate our
> dataset size.
>
> Twenty-five thousand training clips, twenty-five thousand dev clips, and the
> partitions are speaker-disjoint — that matters later.

## 0:45 — 1:30 · Architecture + Architecture diagram

> [Advance through both architecture slides together.]
>
> Five course technologies, each doing real work. MinIO is our object store,
> holding the raw audio. Spark does the heavy batch feature extraction.
> Elasticsearch is the NoSQL store for features, embeddings and predictions.
> Kafka is the streaming backbone. Docker Compose runs the three services.
>
> The one rule that shapes this whole diagram: **feature extraction and the
> model exist in exactly one place each.** `common/features.py` and one saved
> model artifact. The Spark batch job, the Kafka consumer and the web app all
> import the same two things. That's what makes this one AI capability applied
> three ways, instead of three separate features that could silently disagree
> with each other.

## 1:30 — 3:00 · Pipeline: Ingest → Transform → Feature vector → Load

> [Four slides. Keep moving — roughly 20 seconds each.]
>
> Ingest: we parse the dataset's own protocol files and upload every clip to
> MinIO. We talk to it with plain `boto3` — MinIO speaks the S3 API, so this is
> literally the same client code that would talk to AWS.
>
> Transform is the actual big-data step. A Spark RDD of about fifty thousand
> clips, repartitioned so the work spreads across cores. The important detail is
> `mapPartitions`, not `map` — it runs once per Spark task instead of once per
> row, so we build one MinIO client per task rather than fifty thousand of them.
>
> [Feature-vector slide.]
>
> Each clip becomes 53 numbers: twenty MFCCs as mean and standard deviation,
> spectral centroid, bandwidth and rolloff, zero-crossing rate, energy, pitch,
> and duration. Mean and standard deviation rather than raw frames, so every
> clip gets a fixed-length vector no matter how long it is.
>
> Pitch is the intuition worth holding onto — synthetic speech is often
> unnaturally *stable* in pitch. Real humans have vibrato. Machines frequently
> don't.
>
> Load: the feature table goes to Parquet, and the same rows get bulk-indexed
> into Elasticsearch.

## 3:00 — 4:00 · AI capability 1 — classification + How EER is calculated

> We didn't commit to one model. We trained two on identical features — Random
> Forest and Gradient Boosting — and let a metric choose.
>
> We split by the dataset's own train/dev columns, never randomly. A random
> split could put the same speaker on both sides, and then the model learns to
> recognize *that speaker* instead of learning what synthetic audio sounds like.
> Your dev score goes up and your real performance doesn't.
>
> [EER slide.]
>
> We selected on **Equal Error Rate** — the operating point where the false-accept
> rate and the false-reject rate are the same. Two reasons. It's
> threshold-independent, so it can't be gamed by moving a cutoff. And it's the
> metric the official ASVspoof challenge scores on.
>
> Gradient Boosting won: EER 9.3% against Random Forest's 12.3%, ROC-AUC 0.970
> against 0.951.

## 4:00 — 5:30 · Results — the accuracy paradox, found and fixed

> [Slow down. This is the most important slide in the deck.]
>
> This is where the project got interesting, and it's the part I'd most want to
> be asked about.
>
> Our first version reported 92.3% dev accuracy. That sounds like a finished
> project. Then we looked at the per-class breakdown, and it caught 99.8% of
> spoofed audio — but only **28%** of real human speech.
>
> The reason is that dev is about 90% spoof. A model that simply leans toward
> "spoof" scores extremely well on accuracy while being nearly useless at the
> job we actually care about. Accuracy was hiding the failure, not measuring it.
>
> Two separate fixes, and it matters which is which. **Switching models** got
> bonafide recall from 28% to 80% with no threshold change at all — Gradient
> Boosting's probabilities are just far less skewed. **Then switching the
> threshold**, from scikit-learn's default 0.5 to the EER-optimal 0.689, took it
> from 80% to **90.7%**.
>
> And the honest part: overall dev accuracy went *down* when we did that, 94.3%
> to 90.7%. Bonafide precision dropped too. We're calling more things human,
> including some spoofs we used to catch. That's a real trade — you cannot
> maximize both classes at once on imbalanced data, you can only choose where on
> the ROC curve to sit. We chose the principled point instead of the default one.

## 5:30 — 6:30 · Results — ROC, confusion matrix, features, by attack

> [Three results slides, ~20 seconds each.]
>
> The ROC curve shows both candidates with the deployed model's exact operating
> point marked.
>
> Feature importance: `mfcc_3_std` alone carries about 18% — the *variability*
> of a mid-frequency band, which is consistent with the stability intuition from
> earlier.
>
> [Accuracy-by-attack slide.]
>
> By attack type, on 2,000 held-out clips streamed live. Four attack systems we
> catch perfectly — A08, A09, A10 and A13 at 100%. Then A17 at 16%, A19 at 42%,
> A18 at 53%.
>
> And one thing I want to say before anyone asks it. We report **balanced
> accuracy: 89.3%** — 95.3% on real human speech, 83.3% on spoofs. Our raw
> overall accuracy is 84.8%, and we deliberately do *not* lead with it, because
> this set is 88% spoof — so "always guess spoof" would score 88.2% and beat it
> while learning nothing. That's the same trap as the dev-set paradox two slides
> ago, and it turned up again in our own report until we fixed how it's computed.

## 6:30 — 7:00 · AI capability 2 — embeddings & semantic search

> Second capability, same features reused as an embedding — no second model.
>
> One necessary step: raw features live on wildly different scales, duration in
> seconds against rolloff in the thousands. Unscaled cosine similarity would
> just be dominated by the largest-magnitude column. So we standardize, store
> the scaler, and write the vectors into a `dense_vector` field on the index
> that already existed — added in place, no reindex of fifty thousand documents.
>
> Every classification then runs a k-nearest-neighbour search for the five most
> acoustically similar training clips.

## 7:00 — 7:30 · AI capability 3 — streaming enrichment

> Third application: Kafka replays eval clips as events. A consumer fetches each
> from MinIO, extracts the same 53 features, scores it with the same model, and
> writes an enriched prediction into Elasticsearch — one document per clip, as
> it arrives.
>
> This is the part that independently confirmed the threshold fix. Bonafide
> accuracy on never-trained-on eval data came out at 95%, matching what we saw
> on dev. Not the same data the threshold was tuned on.

## 9:15 — 10:00 · Challenges & trade-offs, and close

> Briefly, the things that went wrong, because they're the useful part.
>
> Spark on Windows cost us four separate environment bugs — the wrong Python
> resolving for worker processes, spaces in our project path, a genuine
> pyspark-3.5.1-versus-Python-3.12 incompatibility, and a removed `distutils`.
> None were our logic. All are written up.
>
> And one honest limitation: our streaming consumer ran far slower under
> sustained load than the same code did in isolation. We capped the run at 2,000
> of 5,000 queued clips rather than spend hours on a capability the batch run had
> already validated. We never conclusively root-caused it; the leading candidate
> is Windows throttling long-running background processes.
>
> So — one feature extractor and one model, applied three ways: batch validation,
> streaming enrichment, and an interactive demo. Five course technologies each
> doing real work. And a metric that was quietly lying to us, which we found,
> fixed, and measured twice.
>
> Happy to take questions.

## Anticipated questions

**"Why is A17 so bad?"** A17 is a waveform-filtering voice-conversion attack
whose scores sit closest to the bonafide range, and it's known as the hardest
attack in LA. Our EER threshold buys bonafide recall by giving up margin exactly
there. It's the visible cost of the trade on the paradox slide.

**"84.8% seems low."** Agreed, and raw accuracy is the wrong metric on a set
that's 88% spoof — a trivial always-spoof baseline beats it. Balanced accuracy is
89.3%, and dev EER is 9.3%. Get in front of this rather than defending 84.8%.

**"Would a neural network do better?"** Almost certainly, on score. We chose two
tree ensembles because feature importances stay inspectable and we can explain
every line. That was a deliberate trade of a few points for defensibility.

**"Why not Spark Structured Streaming?"** Per-message scoring is light; it would
have meant pulling in the `spark-sql-kafka` connector for a workload that doesn't
need distributed execution. Spark earns its place in the batch stage.

**"Is 2,000 clips enough?"** For the headline, yes — it barely moved from the
498-clip run (84.9% to 84.8%), so the estimate is stable. The per-attack bars are
the loose ones, at roughly ±6 to ±9 percentage points each.
